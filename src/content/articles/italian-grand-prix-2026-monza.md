An engine change cost Kimi Antonelli thirty grid places at Monza. It put him nineteenth on the grid for his home race, in front of a crowd that had come to watch him and would now watch him spend the afternoon in traffic.

He won.

## The podium

| Pos | Driver | Team |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kimi Antonelli | Mercedes |
| 2 | George Russell | Mercedes |
| 3 | Max Verstappen | Red Bull |

Antonelli became the first Italian driver to win the Italian Grand Prix since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966 — sixty years, at the circuit where that statistic is felt most keenly.

Russell made it a Mercedes one-two. Verstappen took the last podium place, ahead of both McLarens: Lando Norris fourth, Oscar Piastri fifth, the pair of them spending much of the afternoon arguing over the same piece of road.

Lewis Hamilton was sixth, and that sixth place was the only point Ferrari scored all day. Charles Leclerc crashed out on lap three. Pierre Gasly, who had started from pole, finished seventh.

## A pole-sitter in seventh, a nineteenth-place starter in first

That single sentence is the race. Monza rewards slipstreams and punishes cars that cannot follow, and on Sunday the Mercedes could follow anything. Antonelli's recovery was not a matter of luck or a well-timed safety car; it was a car quick enough to make thirty places of penalty a survivable inconvenience.

## The championship after thirteen of twenty-three rounds

| # | Driver | Points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kimi Antonelli | 267 |
| 2 | George Russell | 201 |
| 3 | Lewis Hamilton | 191 |

Antonelli leads Russell by 66 points and Hamilton by 76. Sixty-six points is worth nearly three grands prix. With ten rounds left, it is not yet mathematically decided — but it is the kind of margin that turns a championship fight into a championship procession.

The more interesting question at Monza was not who would win the title. It was whether anybody has anything left to ask of the leader.
