An engine change cost Kimi Antonelli thirty grid places at Monza. It put him nineteenth on the grid for his home grand prix, in front of a crowd that had come to watch him and would now, by every reasonable expectation, watch him spend the afternoon stuck in traffic.

He won.

## The podium

| Pos | Driver | Team |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kimi Antonelli | Mercedes |
| 2 | George Russell | Mercedes |
| 3 | Max Verstappen | Red Bull |

Antonelli became the first Italian driver to win the Italian Grand Prix since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966. Sixty years is a long time to wait anywhere; at Monza, where the crowd has spent six decades cheering for cars rather than countrymen, it is longer still.

Russell completed a Mercedes one-two. Verstappen took the last podium place, and did so ahead of both McLarens — Lando Norris fourth, Oscar Piastri fifth, the pair of them spending a good portion of the afternoon disputing the same piece of road with each other rather than with anyone ahead.

Lewis Hamilton finished sixth. That sixth place was the only point Ferrari scored all day. Charles Leclerc crashed out on lap three, which removed the other half of the team's afternoon before the race had properly settled. Pierre Gasly, who had started from pole position, came home seventh.

## A pole-sitter in seventh, a nineteenth-place starter in first

That single sentence is the race.

Monza is the fastest circuit on the calendar and the one where the slipstream matters most. Long straights and few corners mean a quick car can carry its advantage down the road and use the tow to make passes that would be impossible at, say, Monaco or Hungary. It is the track where a recovery drive is most plausible — and even at Monza, thirty places of penalty is normally a weekend written off.

What made the difference was not luck. There was no fortuitously timed safety car that bunched the field and handed Antonelli a free run at the front. There was a Mercedes quick enough that the penalty became an inconvenience rather than a verdict, and a driver who spent the opening laps taking positions in ones and twos without putting the car anywhere it could be damaged.

Gasly's afternoon tells the other half of the story. Pole position at Monza is worth less than pole almost anywhere else, because the car behind gets the tow and the car in front spends the lap giving it away. Alpine had the single fastest lap of the weekend on Saturday and finished the race four places behind where they started it.

## Ferrari's day

One point, at home, from two cars. Leclerc's lap-three crash ended any chance of a result on the side of the garage that had looked more likely to produce one, and Hamilton's sixth place was a recovery of sorts rather than a performance.

Monza is not a normal race for Ferrari. The tifosi travel, the grandstands are red, and the team's own history at the circuit sets an expectation that no realistic assessment of the car could support this season. The gap between that expectation and one point is where the pressure comes from.

## The championship after thirteen of twenty-three rounds

| # | Driver | Points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kimi Antonelli | 267 |
| 2 | George Russell | 201 |
| 3 | Lewis Hamilton | 191 |

Antonelli leads Russell by 66 points and Hamilton by 76.

Sixty-six points is worth nearly three grands prix. With ten rounds remaining, the title is not mathematically settled — a run of retirements would change the arithmetic quickly, and Formula 1 has produced stranger endings. But a lead that size, held by a driver whose team can win from nineteenth on the grid, is not the shape of a championship fight. It is the shape of a championship procession with ten rounds left to run.

The more interesting question at Monza was not who would win the title. It was whether anybody still has a question to ask of the leader. Russell is the closest thing to a challenger and drives the same car, which means beating Antonelli requires beating him on merit rather than on machinery. Hamilton is in a Ferrari that scored one point at its home race.

## What Monza changes

Practically, very little — the standings moved in the direction they were already moving. Symbolically, rather more. A driver who takes a thirty-place penalty and wins anyway removes the last argument available to the people chasing him: that the gap is a product of circumstance rather than pace.

Ten rounds remain. On this evidence, the season's remaining drama is likely to be found in the fight for second, where Russell and Hamilton are separated by ten points, and in the McLaren garage, where two drivers who spent Sunday racing each other are running out of races in which that is a useful thing to do.
