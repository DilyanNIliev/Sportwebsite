A World Cup final is normally the game where the batting gets careful. On 8 March at the Narendra Modi Stadium in Ahmedabad, India made 255 for 5 and then bowled New Zealand out for 159. Ninety-six runs is not a win, it is a demonstration.

## Three things happened at once

India had already won this competition in 2024. Winning it again in 2026 made them the first team to defend a men's T20 World Cup — in a format built to punish consistency, where twenty overs are short enough for the better side to lose on any given evening.

It was also their third title overall, more than anyone else has won. And they did it at home, which no host had managed before in this tournament. Home advantage in cricket is real and well documented, and it had still never produced a champion until this.

## A tournament, not an innings

Sanju Samson was named player of the tournament, and the numbers explain why better than any description: 321 runs from five innings, at an average of 80.25 and a strike rate of 199.37.

Read those two figures together. A strike rate near 200 usually belongs to somebody who scores fast and gets out — thirty from fifteen balls, then gone. An average of 80 belongs to somebody who stays in. Doing both, across a World Cup, means he was scoring at almost two runs a ball and still only being dismissed once every four innings.

He was not alone. Ishan Kishan made three fifties opening the batting. Hardik Pandya made two, the second of them fifty off twenty-three balls against Zimbabwe.

## What New Zealand ran into

Chasing 256 in a final is close to impossible. New Zealand were not bowled out because they collapsed under the pressure of a tight game; they were bowled out because the required rate was above twelve an over from the first ball, and there is no version of that innings where anyone plays normally.

That is worth being fair about. The final was decided by the first innings, and New Zealand's tournament should not be read from a scoreline produced by a chase that was gone before it started.
